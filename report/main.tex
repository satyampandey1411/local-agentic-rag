\section{The problem and the goal}
Long PDFs such as statutes, budget documents, statistical abstracts and research reports are hard to search.
Hosted chatbots can answer questions about them, but they cost money, send the document to a third party,
and often answer confidently when the PDF does not say. We build for people who cannot or should not upload such files:
law students and small law firms working with statutes and contracts, government and NGO analysts in India with
budget books, statistical abstracts and scanned Hindi or Marathi circulars, researchers, and compliance teams.
Our goal is a local tool that answers questions about \emph{any} uploaded PDF, from a few pages to 10,000 pages,
under four constraints:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Local only.} Open-weight models and no API keys, so the document never leaves the runtime.
  \item \textbf{Free hardware.} One Colab T4: 15 GB GPU memory, 12.7 GB host RAM, 2 vCPUs.
  \item \textbf{Grounded.} Every answer cites the pages it used, or says the document does not contain the answer.
  \item \textbf{Multilingual.} Questions and documents may be in different languages, and scanned pages must work.
\end{itemize}

We started from the reference notebook and an early codebase that had only a non-agentic baseline
(MiniLM search, no tables, no OCR, English only). We rebuilt ingestion, retrieval and the agent, measured
every change on a fixed benchmark, and then scaled ingestion to 10,000 pages and added a multi-user web app.

\section{Approach and architecture}
The system has two paths. \textbf{Ingestion} runs once per PDF and is cached by file hash.
\textbf{Answering} runs per question. A single pass that retrieves, answers and verifies handles most questions,
and the agent loop starts only when that pass refuses or the verifier finds an unsupported claim.

\subsection{Long documents and the web app}
\textbf{Ingestion} is a two-pass stream. Pass 1 reads 16-page batches in parallel worker processes and records document-wide counts
(repeated margin lines, body font size); pass 2 strips headers, chunks and embeds 48-page batches. A page that crashes or hangs the PDF library kills only its
worker: it becomes an empty page with a warning. Both passes checkpoint, so a cancelled job or a closed notebook resumes where it stopped, and
vectors (float16) and the BM25 matrix live on disk.
The \textbf{web app} puts this behind a document library: uploads stream to disk, a background queue shows progress, and several users share one GPU with a visible waiting line.
Answers stream word by word and are then replaced by the verified, cited text; a long document can be questioned once its first batch is indexed;
each citation opens the rendered page with the passage highlighted; an optional access token and upload limits protect a shared server.

\color{navy}Decision & \color{navy}\textbf{Why} & \color{navy}\textbf{Measured effect} \\ \midrule

Table rebuilding & Statistical tables are aligned with spaces, so plain text extraction scrambles rows and columns. & Rows read as \texttt{label | 2025 Q2 = 3.8}. Table questions rose from 42\% to 83\%, helped by better retrieval and table captions \\
BGE-M3 + multilingual reranker & English-only models miss Hindi and Hinglish wording. & Multilingual set: recall@5 0.94 $\to$ 1.00 and MRR 0.88 $\to$ 0.97, with answer accuracy tied \\
Adaptive agent, not an always-on loop & Our first always-on loop was \emph{worse} than a single pass (23 vs 27 of 38): sub-queries crowded out the real evidence. & The loop runs only when needed, with fewer false refusals at similar accuracy \\
Python calculator & The 7B model subtracts and counts wrong even with the right numbers in view. & Arithmetic is exact and operands are checked against the cited row and column, but numerical questions are still 2/6 \\

\textbf{Engineering for a free runtime.} The real limit was host RAM, not the GPU: the Ollama runner (the local LLM server, installed in 41\,s on Colab; 96\,s on Kaggle) grew by about 100\,MB per request until Colab killed the session.
Memory-mapped weights, one request slot, retries and a watchdog that restarts the runner fixed it. In the final run
the watchdog restarted the runner and no question failed. Parsed documents are cached by file hash, so re-runs skip parsing.

\section{Benchmarks}
\textbf{English set:} 61 hand-written questions over three public PDFs: an ACL 2025 paper (17 pages, two columns),
a U.S.\ BEA GDP release (28 pages, mostly statistical tables) and NIST AI 600-1 (64 pages). The questions cover seven categories,
including 9 that the documents cannot answer. A correct answer must contain every required fact and must not refuse.
\textbf{Multilingual set:} 20 questions over a Hindi article, a Marathi article, a scanned (image-only) Hindi document,
and the English paper asked in Hindi and Hinglish. Final results: the project notebook run top to bottom on one T4 (Kaggle, 26 Oct, as Colab's free GPU quota was used up).

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ingestion of table-heavy PDFs is slow on 2 CPUs:} 2.3 to 2.9 pages/s, so 10,000 pages take about 71 min and the 1,224-page Budget Appendix 34 min (37 min for 10,000 pages with the opt-in GPU table detection).
        The table model is about 80\% of that. A faster line-only detector invented tables in academic papers, so it stays off.
  \item \textbf{Section questions on long legal codes.} On the US Code (Title 26) a bold section heading was directly followed by a bold sub-heading and chunks kept only
        the latest, so only 355 sections were identified. Each chunk now records its section number: 1,877 sections, page recall@5 0.75 $\to$ 0.92, answerable 6/12 $\to$ 7/12
        (run 2; not held out, the fix was designed on this set). \S164, \S4081, \S6501 and \S7203 are now correct; 5 of 12 are still wrong (numeric, multi-hop, a definition).
        On a new legal code (US Code Title 21; held-out questions, single use, 27 Oct) 19/25 answerable are correct, 4/5 unanswerable refused, page recall@5 0.917, and 8 of the 9 questions naming a section are correct.
  \item \textbf{Questions that presuppose something absent} (an act passed after the report) are sometimes answered from the nearest passage:
        6 of 9 unanswerable real-document questions were refused. A stricter refusal rule raised false refusals from 3.8\% to 21.2\%, so it was not kept.
  \item \textbf{Small benchmarks.} 61 English and 20 multilingual questions from one annotator; 46 real-document questions; grading matches required facts and can miss a paraphrase.
        The synthetic corpus is template prose, and the real documents are four US federal PDFs.
  \item \textbf{Numerical questions} remain the weakest category (2/6): the 7B model often picks a number from the wrong row or column of a dense table.
  \item \textbf{Coverage.} Hindi, Hinglish and Marathi were measured on 20 questions; OCR on one 2-page scan; charts and figures are not interpreted.
  \item \textbf{Shared use.} The token has no rate limit on guesses, and users behind a proxy share one upload slot. The phone layout was checked at 390\,px in an emulated phone browser, not on a real phone.
\end{itemize}
